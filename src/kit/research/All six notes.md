Fictional practice notes for the AI-First Design Workshop. Not real participants. All six notes in one file, so you can attach it to a chat in one step.

# 01 | Nursing student

Between a lecture and a lab, this student had seven minutes and a paper list of anatomy terms. They chose the first one, wrote a question, and added the answer. Back in the deck, they couldn't find the card. They typed it a second time before looking for a way to check whether the first one had saved. "I don't want to learn the app twice just to make one card."

With a small deck already loaded, they went straight into review. When it was time to leave, they didn't look for a streak. They wanted to know which two terms to come back to before lab. A big percentage didn't help them remember either one. "Tell me what to look at while I'm walking down the hall."

They would import the list of terms, but not a whole deck of someone else's definitions. They turned their paper over and rewrote one definition from memory. That was the part they wanted to do themselves. Typing the same term into two different places was the part they wanted to skip.

# 02 | Designer learning Japanese

This designer keeps words from a Japanese class in a little notebook. To make a card, they copied the kana for *neko* onto the front and put *cat* on the back. Before turning it over, they paused and said the word under their breath. They looked along the edge of the card for something to press. "The first thing I'd add is a way to hear that word."

They opened a dictionary to listen, but the English meaning was right next to the play button. Once they'd seen it, turning over the card felt pointless. Back in the deck, they drew a small speaker beside the kana on a paper sketch. They wanted to try saying the word once on their own, listen to it, and try again. Sound on every new card would get the order backwards.

Some of their cards run the other way: an English cue on the front and Japanese on the back. They covered that back with their hand. "Don't read the part I'm trying to remember." They'd be happy with a plain button if it respected which side was showing.

# 03 | High school teacher

The teacher brought a spreadsheet with a vocabulary term in the first column and an example in the second. They use it for the first five minutes of class. Starting with an empty deck, they copied one row by hand and stopped at the second. "There are twenty more rows and the bell's about to go." Once shown the file import, they flipped two cards to make sure the examples hadn't ended up on the front.

At the projector, they let the class discuss a card before showing the answer. They wouldn't want the app to pronounce a word automatically. That might be a useful clue on Tuesday and give away the exercise on Wednesday. They also covered the end-screen percentage with the projector remote. Different students had answered different cards, so it wasn't anybody's score.

There was a twist when we asked what a good home screen would highlight. The class deck wasn't always the one with the most cards due. They wanted a quick way back to the deck they'd just prepared for that period. "My next class isn't whichever pile is biggest."

# 04 | UX bootcamp grad

Preparing for a portfolio review, this grad wanted to explain a design choice without fumbling for the name of the principle. They had a list of UX laws from class and only a few minutes to practice. On the home screen they opened two decks to find the one they'd used yesterday. Both titles meant something to them. Neither told them which one had cards ready.

They imported the terms, then changed the back of the Fitts's law card. Instead of the class definition, they described the tiny close button in their own prototype. "If I can explain my button, I probably know the law." A fast start helped because it left time to edit, not because they wanted a finished deck.

After marking the familiar cards Easy, they stopped at a percentage above 100. They counted the cards on their fingers and checked whether they'd accidentally reviewed one twice. "Did I get extra credit, or is that counting something else?" They could recall the one example they'd struggled with. They wanted to see that card again, even if it lowered the number.

# 05 | Commuter learning Spanish

This learner reviews phrases on the bus. They picked a card with *¿Me avisa en mi parada?* on the front, waited until the bus was quiet, and tried saying it. They could recognize the phrase but weren't sure where to put the stress. "I can read the whole thing. I want to hear where your voice goes." Hearing the Spanish was the first change they asked for. A generated English explanation wasn't what was missing.

They reached for an earbud, then put it back when their stop was called. On the next card they wanted to read in silence, not leave the session to find a sound setting. If a voice isn't available on their phone, they'd rather keep studying than wait for a download. They practice on this route partly because it works when reception is poor.

When asked how many cards were left, they checked the bus display before checking the app. "I don't need to finish the deck. I need to get off the bus." Knowing they'd done three cards would feel useful even if they had to stop with seven left.

# 06 | Med student who lives in Anki

This student opens Anki before they open their lecture notes. They brought a small practice deck, not their study history. After importing it, they checked the fronts and backs of three familiar cards. They were willing to try a different app with ten cards. They weren't ready to move years of scheduling decisions just because the new home screen looked nicer. "Ten cards is a trial. My whole deck is a commitment."

They understood why a card might come up twice: the second appearance is useful practice. What puzzled them was a finish screen that didn't say whether it was counting different cards or times they'd answered. They sketched three boxes on paper, then drew a second tick in one of them. They'd accept either number if they knew which one they were looking at.

The student made a point of leaving a few cards due when they closed the app. On a busy day, a short review they could repeat tomorrow was better than wiping the queue clean. "Don't turn my backlog into a grade." A deck recommendation was welcome as long as they could choose another one.
